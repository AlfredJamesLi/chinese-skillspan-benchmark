\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Online job advertisements describe the knowledge and skills employers seek and provide evidence of changing occupational requirements~\citep{ILO_2020_OJVs_BigData,senger2024survey}. Document-level methods retrieve skills from predefined inventories~\citep{bhola2020retrieving}, whereas span extraction locates requirement mentions in the source text~\citep{gnehm2022skills}. Job Skill Named Entity Recognition (JobSkillNER) combines span identification with type assignment. Preserving source wording allows readers to inspect span boundaries and types in context.

Even short Chinese phrases can require multiple decisions about span boundaries and types. A technology name may refer to tool use in one clause and knowledge of underlying principles in another. Coordinated expressions may describe separate activities, but an action and its objects must remain together when splitting would change the requirement. Chinese text generally lacks spaces between words, and tokenization alone cannot resolve these semantic boundaries~\citep{peng2015weibo,chen2021boundary}. Although similar ambiguities occur in other languages~\citep{nguyen2024rethinking}, translating a dataset alone does not determine which Chinese substrings to annotate. Explicit, context-sensitive annotation rules are needed.

Existing resources provide useful foundations, but their annotation schemes and prediction targets differ. English SkillSpan permits overlapping SKILL and KNOWLEDGE annotation layers~\citep{zhang2022skillspan}. Kompetencer adds fine-grained labels derived from the European Skills, Competences, Qualifications and Occupations (ESCO) classification to annotated Danish and English spans through distant supervision~\citep{jensen2022kompetencer}. Work on German job advertisements extracts education, experience, and language requirements and maps their skill content to taxonomy concepts~\citep{gnehm2022skills}. Chinese recruitment research includes skill and specialty annotation for occupational profiling~\citep{cao2021occupational} and skill-demand forecasting~\citep{chen2024jobsdf}. Building on these resources, we focus on explicit conventions for Chinese span boundaries and competency types, together with a shared evaluation protocol.

We introduce Chinese-SkillSpan, a benchmark for competency span extraction in Chinese job advertisements (Figure~\ref{fig:macro-micro}). The original source corpus contains 22,840 sentences from four recruitment collections, while the human reference set contains 150 sentences and 663 annotated spans. The handbook distinguishes language (L), knowledge-related requirements (K), occupational skills (S), and transversal competencies (T). We use \emph{competency spans} to denote contiguous source-text mentions across all four categories and reserve \emph{occupational skills} for category S. The annotation scheme draws on ESCO's high-level categories and the knowledge--skill distinction in the European Qualifications Framework~\citep{ESCOv1_2_2024,council2017eqf}, but does not assign ESCO concept identifiers. The project-specific K category covers substantive knowledge, qualifications, and explicit industry-background requirements.

We compare prompted language models, Qwen fine-tuning with model-generated silver labels, and multilingual encoders under the shared evaluation protocol. Under fixed inference settings, silver-supervised Qwen adaptation improves end-to-end extraction relative to the same unadapted model. The human reference set focuses on ambiguous cases considered during handbook development and contains distinct sentences from documents also represented in the supervision data. A separate blinded study assesses annotation agreement after individual self-review, with closer agreement on boundaries alone than on boundaries and types together. We document annotation layers, evaluation targets, and procedures following guidance on dataset documentation and annotation agreement~\citep{bender2018datastatements,gebru2021datasheets,artstein2008agreement}.

An earlier preprint presented a preliminary account of Chinese-SkillSpan~\citep{li2026chineseskillspanpreprint}. The present article substantially revises its resource definitions, annotation procedures, and evaluation; \hyperref[app:d]{Appendix D} distinguishes the historical reports from the experiments documented here.

The study makes three contributions. First, we provide a Chinese competency-span resource with character-level offsets and separate human reference and silver annotation layers. Second, we develop a handbook that specifies context-sensitive boundary and type rules with positive and negative examples. Third, we provide annotation-quality assessments and model baselines under shared scoring procedures, with analyses of model errors in span boundaries and types.
