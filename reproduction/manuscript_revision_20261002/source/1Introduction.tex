\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Job markets keep changing, and so do the requirements that employers write into their advertisements~\citep{ILO_2020_OJVs_BigData,senger2024survey}. Advertisements record which competencies are asked for, and extracting them from unstructured text is one way to make that record usable. Span extraction does this by marking requirement mentions in the source text, so a reader can check where each span begins and ends and what type it received~\citep{gnehm2022skills}. Document-level methods take a different route and assign skills from a \mbox{predefined inventory~\citep{bhola2020retrieving}.}

Existing resources differ in annotation schemes and prediction targets (Table~\ref{tab:related-span}). English SkillSpan permits overlapping SKILL and KNOWLEDGE layers~\citep{zhang2022skillspan}. Kompetencer adds fine-grained labels derived from the European Skills, Competences, Qualifications and Occupations (ESCO) classification to annotated Danish and English spans through distant supervision~\citep{jensen2022kompetencer}, and work on German-language job advertisements links requirement spans to taxonomy concepts~\citep{gnehm2022skills}. Chinese recruitment studies cover skill and specialty annotation~\citep{cao2021occupational} and skill-demand forecasting~\citep{chen2024jobsdf}. Chinese adds further difficulties. The text has no spaces between words, so tokenisation alone cannot show an annotator where a competency span begins and ends~\citep{peng2015weibo,chen2021boundary}. An action must stay with its objects whenever splitting them would change the requirement. Explicit, context-sensitive conventions for Chinese boundaries and types are therefore needed for consistent annotation.

We introduce Chinese-SkillSpan, a benchmark of source-text spans with character-level offsets (Figure~\ref{fig:macro-micro}). We use \emph{competency span} for any contiguous mention of a requirement within the handbook's scope in the source text, and reserve \emph{occupational skills} for one of its types. The handbook assigns each span to one of four types: language (L), knowledge-related requirements (K), occupational skills (S), or transversal competencies (T). The types draw on ESCO's high-level categories and on the knowledge--skill distinction in the European Qualifications Framework~\citep{ESCOv1_2_2024,council2017eqf}, but the scheme assigns no ESCO concept identifiers. Human reference labels for evaluation are kept separate from machine-generated silver labels for supervision. Table~\ref{tab:guide-types} gives handbook examples.

We compare prompted language models, the Chinese recruitment model JobBERT-zh + CRF, a Qwen model adapted with silver labels, and multilingual encoders under a shared evaluation protocol. Under fixed inference settings, silver-label adaptation improves end-to-end extraction over the same model without adaptation. Among the configurations evaluated, a prompted GPT model ranks highest, without a clear advantage over the next-highest configuration. The human reference set was selected from sentences where model annotations disagreed, so it concentrates on ambiguous cases that came up during handbook development. In a separate small blinded study, agreement on span boundaries alone was slightly higher than agreement that also required types. We document the annotation layers, evaluation targets, and procedures, following guidance on dataset documentation and annotation agreement~\citep{bender2018datastatements,gebru2021datasheets,artstein2008agreement}.

An earlier preprint gave a preliminary account of Chinese-SkillSpan~\citep{li2026chineseskillspanpreprint}; this article substantially revises its resource definitions, annotation procedures, and evaluation, and \hyperref[app:d]{Appendix D} separates the earlier reports from the experiments reported here.

\Needspace{2\baselineskip}
This study makes three contributions. First, it provides a Chinese competency-span resource with character-level offsets, and it keeps human reference and silver annotation layers separate. Second, it develops a handbook with context-sensitive boundary and type rules, illustrated with positive and negative examples. Third, it reports annotation-quality assessments and model baselines under shared scoring procedures, shows that silver-label adaptation improves extraction, and analyses the boundary and type errors that models make.
