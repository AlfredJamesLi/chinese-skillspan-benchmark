\subsection{Task Definition and Label Schema}

Given a Chinese job-ad sentence $x$, the task is to predict non-overlapping spans $(s,e,t)$. Here, $s$ and $e$ are zero-based, end-exclusive Unicode-code-point offsets, and $t\in\{L,K,S,T\}$. Each span must reproduce the contiguous substring $x[s:e]$, including its spelling and internal spaces. The annotation describes a requirement in the advertisement; it does not measure an applicant's ability.

Figure~\ref{fig:macro-micro} summarizes how annotation supports model comparison and agreement assessment. The handbook assigns L to language requirements, including language names, proficiency levels, examinations, and certificates. K covers facts, principles, theories, and fields of study. S covers occupational actions, methods, and tool use. T covers transversal capabilities such as communication, cooperation, cognition, and self-management. These categories draw on ESCO and on the distinction between knowledge and skills in the European Qualifications Framework~\citep{ESCOv1_2_2024,council2017eqf}.

The project K label includes substantive knowledge and recruitment proxies: educational qualifications, non-language qualifications, and explicit industry-background requirements. These proxies are an operational extension, not an assertion that credentials or experience measure knowledge under ESCO or the EQF. Accordingly, K scores measure extraction of this combined project category. The frozen labels do not identify these subtypes, so a knowledge-only evaluation requires an additional human review. The boundary and context rules below define the operational task.

\subsection{Annotation Guidelines and Adjudication}
\label{sec:annotation-guidelines-r36}

The guidelines address three decisions that a list of skill terms cannot settle: whether a clause is eligible, how much text to retain, and which type fits its context. Annotation begins by determining whether a clause expresses a requirement for the candidate. Benefits, applicant-side procedures, company promotion, and pure outcomes do not qualify on their own; recruitment activities performed as occupational duties are assessed separately. Mixed clauses are considered separately. A sentence with no competency mention receives an empty annotation; insufficient context or an unresolved interpretation is recorded as uncertainty.

We adapted SkillSpan's semantic-completeness and coordination principles for Chinese annotation~\citep{zhang2022skillspan}. Our handbook asks annotators to select the shortest source span that preserves the complete requirement, without a fixed length cap. Generic proficiency triggers usually remain outside the span, but a modal can remain inside a complete colloquial capability expression. Necessary action heads and complements are retained. Coordinated activities are split only when each remains interpretable; a shared action and its objects stay together (Figure~\ref{fig:illustrative-annotations}).

\input{tex/representative_annotations_main}

Experience expressions are assessed in three steps. Annotators first distinguish a named occupational activity from generic tenure or industry background. Generic duration alone is excluded; explicit industry-background requirements receive the project K proxy label. For an activity, the annotator then removes the experience qualifier provisionally and checks whether the remaining words still name the required activity in context. If only an event name or incomplete fragment remains, the qualifier is retained. Finally, coordinated items are split only when each passes the same check. An internet-company operations activity is S; a requirement for an internet-industry background is K. Appendix~A provides contrasting examples.

Type depends on what the clause requires: using SQL supports S, whereas knowledge of SQL principles supports K. A predicate can determine type without being part of the selected span. Familiarity alone does not decide type. General communication and coordination remain T inside duty clauses, whereas technical operations receive S. Language examinations receive L; educational and non-language qualifications receive K. Figure~\ref{fig:illustrative-annotations} shows original corpus examples with their archived annotations, and \hyperref[app:a]{Appendix A} gives further examples and rule sources.

Adjudication treats scope, boundaries, and type as separate decisions~\citep{artstein2008agreement}. There is no total priority order among L, K, S, and T. Type uncertainty is recorded separately from accepted labels. The current handbook permits provisional S in a working adjudication record, but requires the entire unresolved record to be excluded from final training targets. This bookkeeping convention does not license assigning S by default in the accepted data. Same-boundary alternatives, nested candidates, and unresolved overlaps are kept in the adjudication record rather than duplicated in the flat annotation layer. \hyperref[app:a]{Appendix A} gives the full rules and their history. The displayed corpus examples retain historical annotations; they are distinguished from current-handbook illustrations in Appendix~A. Each completed experiment retains its original labels.

\subsection{Human Annotation and LLM-Assisted Labeling}
\label{sec:annotation-workflow-r36}

Human annotation and adjudication established the reference labels and informed the handbook. The authors used the official OpenAI Codex interface for adopted Silver annotation; Codex names the interface, while Table~\ref{tab:model-identity} identifies recorded model names and roles. Initial sample review preceded bulk annotation. Automated checks verified substrings, offsets, labels, and coverage; human review addressed interpretation.

Bulk annotation used the handbook available at generation time. Later amendments were recorded separately; completed experiments remained unchanged.

\subsection{Ethics and Data Use}
The study analyzed recruitment advertisements and did not recruit job applicants or collect applicant responses. Annotation and review were performed by research-team members. Institutional ethics approval was not sought; no formal exemption is claimed. The authors confirm permission to use the source materials for academic research. Permissions to conduct research and rights to redistribute third-party recruitment text are recorded separately in the component-specific access terms. Privacy screening of the released text is reported in Appendix~D.
