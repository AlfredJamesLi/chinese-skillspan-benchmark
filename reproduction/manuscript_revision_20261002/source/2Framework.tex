\subsection{Competency Definitions and Annotation Schema}

Competency research connects people's capabilities with the activities they perform. \citet{mcclelland1973competence} argued for assessing competence through behavior relevant to meaningful performance criteria. ESCO defines competence in terms of demonstrated use of knowledge, skills, and personal, social, or methodological abilities in work and learning, with responsibility and autonomy~\citep{escopediaCompetence}. Here, we use \emph{competency} as an umbrella term for capabilities and associated requirements expressed in Chinese recruitment text.

ESCO provides a vocabulary for describing these requirements across occupations. Its skills pillar distinguishes knowledge concepts from skill/competence concepts, while grouping skills and competences together~\citep{escopediaSkillsPillar}. The European Qualifications Framework defines knowledge through what is learned about a field, including its facts, principles, theories, and practices. Skills concern putting knowledge and practical know-how to use in performing tasks and addressing problems~\citep{council2017eqf}. This knowledge--application distinction also informs SkillSpan's skill and knowledge annotations~\citep{zhang2022skillspan}.

Chinese-SkillSpan uses ESCO as a conceptual basis for four task-specific annotation categories~\citep{ESCOv1_2_2024}. Language requirements receive L, knowledge-related requirements K, occupational skills S, and transversal competencies T. L separates natural-language requirements, while T covers broadly applicable communication, cooperation, self-management, and related dispositions. The project-specific K category includes qualifications and explicit industry-background requirements alongside substantive knowledge. Table~\ref{tab:guide-types} summarizes the categories with bilingual examples; the handbook guides their application to Chinese spans.

Given a Chinese job-advertisement sentence $x$, the task is to predict a flat set of non-overlapping spans $(s,e,t)$, where $t\in\{L,K,S,T\}$. Offsets are measured in Unicode code points: $s$ denotes the zero-based start position, and $e$ denotes the exclusive end position. Each annotated mention must match the contiguous substring $x[s:e]$ exactly, including its spelling and internal spaces.

\subsection{Annotation Guidelines and Adjudication}
\label{sec:annotation-guidelines-r36}

The handbook specifies what constitutes a requirement, which source characters belong to its span, and how context determines its type. Scope is assessed clause by clause: benefits, application procedures, company promotion, and outcomes alone are excluded, while recruitment activities can qualify as occupational duties. A confirmed absence of in-scope requirements receives an empty annotation. Missing context or unresolved interpretation is handled separately, including in sentences that mix requirements with other content.

Boundary selection prioritizes semantic completeness. Following SkillSpan~\citep{zhang2022skillspan}, annotators retain the shortest complete source span, with no fixed length limit or added words. Generic proficiency triggers are usually excluded; modal verbs, heads, and complements remain when needed to express the capability. Coordinated items are split only when each remains meaningful in context; shared actions stay with their objects when needed to preserve the requirement.

Type assignment uses the requirement's context. Using SQL supports S for the tool mention, whereas understanding the principles of SQL supports K for the complete knowledge phrase. General communication and coordination receive T even when stated as duties, while technical operations receive S. Experience expressions follow the same contextual approach: generic duration alone is excluded, explicit industry-background requirements receive K, and an experience qualifier is retained when its removal would change the requirement.

Adjudication resolves scope, boundaries, and type separately~\citep{artstein2008agreement}. Records with unresolved annotation decisions are excluded from labeled training; alternative or overlapping spans remain separate from final non-overlapping annotations. Figure~\ref{fig:illustrative-annotations} pairs human-reference annotations with English translations; \hyperref[app:a]{Appendix A} gives rules G1--G15 and examples.

\subsection{Annotation Process and Quality Assessment}
\label{sec:annotation-workflow-r36}

The human reference set was selected from sentences flagged for disagreement between model annotations, to examine ambiguous requirements. According to the authors' reconstruction of the initial stage, the 50 calibration sentences were annotated separately with machine suggestions available and subsequently adjudicated. A separate 100-sentence challenge sample was collaboratively annotated and reviewed. Both samples informed handbook development, and their finalized labels form the 150-sentence reference used for model evaluation (Figure~\ref{fig:coding-detail}).

Silver labels were generated with GPT-6 Astra through OpenAI Codex, following the handbook and an initial sample review. Automated checks assessed annotation structure and record coverage, including span text, offsets, and labels; sampled human review assessed whether the annotations captured the requirements. Additional assisted annotation used suggestions from GPT-6 Astra, Grok, Qwen, DeepSeek, and Kimi (\hyperref[tab:annotation-models]{Appendix C}). Human annotation and review cover 550 distinct sentences across the reference, silver-review samples, and blinded study (\hyperref[sec:human-review-coverage]{Appendix B}).

The handbook was refined across annotation stages; \hyperref[sec:guide-version-history]{Appendix A.4} identifies the versions associated with each study. Reported scores use the preserved labels, prompts, and evaluation inputs. Dated Chinese and English editions are available in the \href{https://github.com/AlfredJamesLi/chinese-skillspan-benchmark/blob/main/notes/handbooks/README.md}{handbook index}. The latest reviewed edition is intended for new annotation; reproduction requires the original study materials.

\subsection{Ethics and Data Use}
The study analyzed recruitment advertisements, with annotation and review conducted by members of the research team. It did not recruit job applicants or collect applicant responses. Institutional ethics approval was not sought, and no formal exemption is claimed. The authors confirm permission to use the source materials for academic research. Component-specific terms distinguish research use from redistribution rights; \hyperref[app:d]{Appendix D} describes automated privacy screening and the human review required before a privacy-vetted release.
